Teaching Science to Children, A Chemistry Club

Introduction

Science is exciting! I defended that position throughout Year 12 and Year 13 by starting, running and organising “Chemistry Club” with a friend. Since leaving school I have been working at Sci-Tech - a science camp for children aged 5-14. This commentary explores what I have learnt in my experiences teaching children, and how practicals can be best utilised in this context, as well as my experience pursuing science throughout Secondary Education in the UK - a retrospective view from an Undergraduate student. Included below are the materials I produced to deliver my lessons.

Discussion

The Curse of Knowledge is the phenomenon where people with high levels of expertise or experience struggle to teach or communicate with beginners. I have experienced this first-hand at university. At Oxford, alongside lectures students have tutorials with an expert in the field. These typically comprise of one professor (or PhD candidate) and two students (partners). The best tutorials (I have found) are ones where the student is forced to teach themselves. Rather than the professor depositing knowledge onto the students, the student is prompted to discover knowledge from the professor - learning with someone rather than from someone.

This “Socratic Method” is shown to be far more effective and avoids the implication of ‘the curse’ and allows the student to experience the thrill of scientific discovery. This can be, and should be applied when teaching children science.

Children are naturally curious, but are often impatient and lack motivation to learn. I noticed that this lack of motivation to learn is most prevalent from the ages of 11-14 (tweenhood). This is when I observed children stating (as the silly billies they are) they were, “too cool, or too old” for things, another reason why interactive learning is incredibly important. It keeps these children engaged, and allows them to adapt their perception of the task to what they believe is “cool enough” and “mature enough” for them.

Incorporating practicals is especially important for catching the attention of children. Showing them something flashy, or even better, allowing them to do it themselves, motivates them to learn the underlying principles. Students seemed to enjoy my chemistry club more, when I adopted the:

Introduce → Start Experiment → Start Discuss → Finish Experiment → Finish Discussion

format, over:

Experiment → Explain (or) Explain → Experiment

When working at Sci-Tech we often adjusted the order of when practicals and demonstrations were given during our chemistry day throughout the camp. This was done such that children stay engaged throughout (and also so that I, a person with only two hands, can prepare all the materials). The:

Introduce + Demo → Interactive Activity + Explanation → Discuss Further + Demo → Interactive Activity + Conclusion

format was most popular. The initial demo excited children and motivated them to take part in the interactive activity, and the interactive activity motivated them to pay attention during the subsequent demo which kept them engaged throughout the final interactive activity despite being tired at the end of the day. This technique of interleaving of events and activities with bouts of information creates associations between content and events. This creates a learning network which improves memory.

This is a Network Model of Learning as opposed to a Linear Model of Learning. This format can be applied during the duration of a day, or week, or in my case at university, over years. The undergraduate course at chemistry involves lectures, tutorials and labs which do not always follow in chronological procession. Consequently, you encounter several concepts before the relationships between them are made explicit. This places a greater demand on independent learning, and can often generates significant workload as you try to maintain and develop multiple emerging ideas simultaneously.

However, this apparent fragmentation may also have an important educational benefit: it allows numerous conceptual nodes to develop in parallel, which can subsequently become interconnected through later lectures, practical experiences, tutorials, and independent study. Rather than constructing knowledge as a series of small, self-contained sequences, you progressively develop a broader and more densely connected conceptual network. I have found such a network is ultimately more robust, because individual concepts are supported by multiple connections to prior knowledge and experience, reducing the likelihood that isolated knowledge will weaken or be forgotten over time.

Conclusion

Conscientiousness (big word that) surrounding The Curse of Knowledge, and formatting in accordance to The Network Model of Learning have improved my interactions with children significantly when attempting to teach them science. It is also important to note that selecting practicals that are both age appropriate and engaging is crucial. Shown below are the experiments, and the corresponding resources I used to deliver my lessons in Chemistry Club when I was a student at my sixth form college. When creating these resources I tried to maintain a consistent theme of creativity throughout. This is because in secondary education I often felt that I was forced to choose between being creative-artsy or being logical-scientific. This is fallacy. Creativity is not only compatible with, but required for scientific development. However, I had not yet developed a “style.” So, some are inconsistently better than others (sorry). The presentations were designed to last the duration of 30-40 minutes. I hope these can be useful to at least one person 😊.
