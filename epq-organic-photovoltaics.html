Abstract, Introduction, Discussion, Conclusion & References

Is there a possibility for Organic Photovoltaics to match or replace Silicon Variants? By: Franka Stefanie Iliffe Date: 25/03/24

Abstract This project evaluates the efficacy of emerging thin-film organic photovoltaics (OPVs) against established silicon photovoltaics (SiPVs) across five key categories: sustainability, manufacturing, lifespan and stability, energy efficiency, and commercial applications. Utilising a self-constructed computational model tailored to Manchester, UK's energy demand, the effectiveness of each technology is analysed to provide insight into their integration within the region. Drawing from secondary research, this EPQ assesses whether OPVs can rival or replace SiPVs while examining their potential role in leveraging solar technology to mitigate carbon emissions and meet global energy needs while highlighting the challenges in improving and commercialising OPVs. For useful contextual definitions accompanied by a list of abbreviations see Appendix B. Introduction Interest in renewable energy has been gaining momentum in the effort to alleviate the effects of fossil fuels. While research into improving energy efficiency and conservation will improve the global energy outlook, it remains clear that investments in alternative energy resources are a requirement if we want to decarbonise our future and satisfy energy demand (Mazzio, K.A. and Luscombe, C.K. 2014). Photovoltaics is the branch of technology concerned with the conversion of light into electric current. Solar panels use the photovoltaic effect, first demonstrated by Becquerel in 1839, to harvest energy directly from sunlight making solar energy an infinitely available source of clean energy. Being easy to install and maintain means solar panels lend themselves to use in a variety of different locations and applications (Solak, E.K. and Irmak, E. 2023). It is evident that photovoltaics (PVs) will play a prevalent role in the future renewable energy mix, forming the basis of the rationale behind this work (Mazzio, K.A. and Luscombe, C.K. 2014). This project was inspired by the work of Oxford Photovoltaics, a company which has constructed solar panels with record efficiencies, alongside the work of Heliatek, a German company focused on developing a new, more sustainable, organic solar solution with low carbon footprints. Throughout the discussion, I attempt to reconcile the need for high efficiency with the desire to maintain low eco-toxicities. To ensure objectivity and mitigate potential biases inherent in industry-affiliated research, this study draws primarily from peer-reviewed literature from esteemed publications such as those of the Royal Society of Chemistry and American Physical Society. While numerical data predating 2020 has been omitted, qualitative insights from earlier years are integrated into the discussion. Progressing into the discussion, acknowledging the subjectivity inherent in assigning value to quantitative data is important in considering diverse metrics and perspectives. Such considerations will inform the

analysis and conclusion regarding the viability and merits of emerging thin-film OPVs vis-à-vis established SiPVs, particularly in the discussed contexts. Discussion 1. Sustainability Within this section, the environmental impact of each technology is scrutinised at the outset of the project, underscoring the central theme that permeates the entirety of this work, to ensure coherence and relevance from start to finish. It is important to appreciate that quantifying the sustainability of a PV module is extremely complex, as it requires careful consideration of the product’s entire lifecycle. (McCulloch, I. et al. 2023) With this in mind, the environmental impact of a device is often measured using carbon dioxide equivalents, especially in discussing carbon emission factors (Müller, S. and Kube, S. 2020). The carbon emission factor of a device is dependent on power generation over a module’s lifetime. Per kWh, SiPVs emit approximately 40g of CO2 while OPVs release double that at 80g/kWh. However, this value is expected to decrease to less than 5g/kWh while that of their silicon counterparts is not expected to go below 10g/kWh in the foreseeable future (Darling, S.B. and You, F. 2013). In the carbon evaluation of Heliatek’s organic panels, 15 kg of CO2 was emitted over their entire lifecycle per kWh of electricity, one-third that of a silicon module. (McCulloch, I. et al. 2023). Therefore, each kWh generated by HeliaSol panels saves upwards of 678g CO2e. This means HeliaSol panels have an exceptionally low carbon footprint of 3-15 CO2e/kWh while their silicon counterparts have values of 80 on average. The energy consumed in the production of these panels contributes 25% to the resulting CO2e values. Due to OPVs being low-weight and highly flexible, they are exceptionally easy to package and transport so these processes only contribute 7% (Müller, S. and Kube, S. 2020). Silicon panels are heavy and rigid so they cannot be transported, stored or packaged as sustainably and efficiently. Unfortunately, some companies choose to use carcinogenic solvents in OPV manufacture. This is typically avoided by transitioning to alternative green solvents used in other cell technologies. Despite this, OPV modules still have an eco-toxicity lower than SiPVs. The largest impacting factor regarding the eco-toxicity of SiPVs is the usage of hazardous gases and liquids in production. Furthermore, lead is used in small quantities for contact formation and soldering. The organic solar solution is free of these toxic heavy metals (Heinrich, M. et al 2020). The development of low-cost printing methods to develop OPVs means they are cheaper and easier to produce than their silicon counterparts. Silicon modules suffer drawbacks such as large thermal budgets, relatively slow manufacturing speeds and energy-intensive production processing, with the manufacturing of either module being analysed in the subsequent section (Solak & Irmak, 2023).

The end-of-life treatment of solar panels is essential as they cannot be reused. OPVs are easy to dispose of as they are made of decomposable, non-toxic materials, such as carbon-based polymers (Solak & Irmak, 2023). However, silicon takes upwards of 50 years to decompose (Solak & Irmak, 2023). Flexible polymer-based OPVs offer the potential for energy recovery upon incineration. This energy can be harvested to produce new OPVs in a closed loop with the metallically based portion of the film being extracted from the resulting ash and recycled. Occasionally, many companies choose to adopt acid-based recycling for the recovery of metals. However, these processes are associated with 20% higher environmental impacts than ash extraction (Müller, S. and Kube, S. 2020).Still, from this evidence, we can conclude that organic panels are favourable when considering the environmental impact of either module due to their exceptionally low carbon footprint and favourable end-of-life treatment. 2. Manufacturing OPVs and SiPVs are the only solar technologies that incorporate a sufficiently small amount of rare earth metals for this to not prevent them from scaling up to Tera-watt capacity (Darling, S.B. and You, F. 2013). Therefore, neither module faces the problem of the potential unavailability of critical elements (Darling, S.B. and You, F. 2013). However, Indium-tin oxide is incorporated into the electrodes of some OPV cells, its limited availability and the brittleness of indium’s compounds make it unsuitable for large-scale OPV production (Riede, M. et al 2020). As an alternative, graphite can be exfoliated to graphene using a plasma-spray technique for use in fully carbon-transparent electrodes, offering a pathway to a lower carbon footprint (McCulloch, I. et al. 2023). The required inorganic and organic materials for both panels are exceptionally abundant. However, obtaining silicon is complicated. The silicon is mined and then separated from undesired materials to form a concentrate (American Physical Society, 2011). The extraction of silicon from silicon dioxide (shown below) releases CO2 contributing to the high carbon footprint of silicon production. Additionally, the silicon produced is metallurgical grade, with a level of purity (~99%) that is insufficient for solar devices. Thus, further processing is still required to purify the element. Silicon is reacted with hydrochloric acid and purified in multiple distillations. The SiHCl3 produced attacks stainless steel, so equipment must be replaced frequently. Purified SiHCl3 is then reduced using hydrogen, producing a high-purity silicon rod which is maintained at 1,150oC by passing an electric current through it - the production of silicon is highly energy intensive as a result. Single-crystal ingots are then produced in the Czochralski process where the ingots are sliced into wafers. This is very slow and results in 25% material loss. The sliced material must then be polished and further cell fabrication is still required beyond this stage (Tao, M. 2008).

Nonetheless , this method does result in exceptionally pure silicon wafers for maximum open-circuit voltage. In comparison, the highly disordered amorphous nature and impurity of organic materials used in organic semiconductors (OSCs) means they have a much lower maximum open-circuit voltage (Kippelen, B. and Brédas, J.L. 2009). Still, the fabrication of SiPVs with thin silicon wafers is far more challenging with high wafer breakage rates up to module installation, further increasing cost. Many tool adjustments are required to handle these thin wafers and this may not be feasible long-term (Liu, Z. et al. 2019), especially with the quality of the entire wafer being imperative for charge carrier lifetime and mobility, which determines device efficiency. The large quantity of silicon required in the panels creates a shortage of materials whenever the industry accelerates, despite silicon’s abundance (Tao, M. 2008). However, experience and familiarity with the distribution of mineral deposits indicate there is no absolute limit on the availability of any chemical element.Claims in popular literature that indicate this, are a result of the misunderstanding of terms such as resources and reserves (American Physics Society, 2011).This means that if we were to find an easier method of manufacturing silicon fit for solar panels, we can meet demand even with industry accelerations. However, there is unlikely to be a suitable replacement for mining, which has a profound impact on the surrounding natural habitat. Comparably, OPVs are far easier to manufacture. Solution-based techniques are well-suited for organic thin film deposition and they can be done under atmospheric conditions, minimising expense (Shaheen, S.E. et al 2011). Still, OPVs have a lower production volume compared to silicon panels and are produced in small quantities, making them less commercially viable for large-scale production (Solak, E.K. and Irmak, E. 2023). The polymers used in OPV fabrication are inexpensive and renewable. However, polymer synthesis suffers from batch-to-batch variation in source materials, resulting in inconsistent performance. Smaller molecules have been considered as substitutes for polymers in devices. They are synthesised more consistently and exhibit long-range order, which allows for higher electron mobilities. In some cases, these molecules are soluble enough to be solution-processed, which is vital for OPV success. However, the effect of the solvent type used in solution-phase deposition methods on cell performance is poorly understood and will inhibit further development in the manufacture of OPVs. Nonetheless, the alternative vapour deposition processes are more expensive (Darling, S.B. and You/F. 2013, Mazzio, K.A. and Luscombe, C.K. 2014). If we want to scale up OPV production and improve throughput, we will require an alternative to the current spin coating techniques because of the larger volume of materials and solvent areas required (Mazzio, K.A. and Luscombe, C.K. 2014). New roll-to-roll (R2R) coating techniques with heat recovery, closed cooling circuits and solvent-free processes have been developed in response (Müller, S. and Kube, S. 2020). These

methods have no loss mechanisms and are exceptionally efficient for these stages of early development. With the use of machine learning, OPV modules with efficiencies of 10.2% have been successfully fabricated. These processes open up pathways to reduce production-related requirements for both energy and materials. However, silicon production is an established process and translating this technology for industrial-scale production is challenging (Kippelen, B. and Brédas, J.L. 2009/Müller, S. and Kube, S. 2020). The prospects of future development with the promise of successful outcomes means OPVs may one day dominate the market, provided that the necessary challenges are overcome such as, improving the scalability of the discussed processes and improving device lifespan and efficiencies. These parameters will be discussed in the sections following. 3. Lifespan and Stability This section will explore the crucial aspects of PV module stability and lifespan. To successfully enter the market, PV technologies must offer lifespans that make them attractive for purchase and suitable for various applications. The organic materials used in OPVs are not air-stable. In the comparisons of vacuum-processed and solution-processed OPVs, the former showed greater stabilities. The materials used in this technique are typically of high purity. They undergo thermal evaporation removing side-chains, which are most prone to degradation. Moreover, these modules tend to exhibit higher morphological stabilities because molecules tend to find near-equilibrium structures during film growth. This is important because any changes in the molecular arrangement within the active layer can affect device performance (Riede, M. et al 2020). The photodegradation of electrodes, buffer layers and semiconducting polymers is triggered by water and oxygen, meaning the performance of OPVs worsens over time with exposure to UV light and moisture. Consequently, local weather conditions have a great effect on the device's lifetime. To increase lifetime, access to oxygen and water to active regions is limited by encapsulation (Solak, E.K. and Irmak, E. 2023). Glass-glass encapsulation is common in lab settings and may be viable for integrated panels in glass facades. This is not an option for other markets, where cost, low weight and mechanical flexibility are their primary benefits (Riede, M. et al 2020). Efficiency tends to decrease as temperature rises, and frequent temperature fluctuations can lead to cell degradation. However, this effect is less pronounced in OPVs - they generally operate at lower temperatures compared to SiPVs. Therefore, OPVs may be well-suited for use in cooler climates. Additionally, silicon panels are also more vulnerable to damage from wind impact and often crack under mechanical load (Heinrich, M. et al 2020). So far, polymer-based OPVs have had lifetimes of 6 years reported, with HeliaSol panels recording lifetimes of 20 years with their small molecule processes (Darling, S.B. and You, F. 2013). OPVs which incorporate non-fullerene acceptors (NFAs) have prolonged lifetimes, as well as promising photochemical and thermal stabilities (Riede, M. et al 2020). NFAs also absorb light more strongly in

contrast to traditional fullerenes, which positively impacts device efficiency. Efficiency is discussed in further detail later on (Lowrie, W. et al. 2023). Still, it is impossible to ignore that commercially available SiPVs already offer a guaranteed power output for up to 30 years. To overcome setbacks concerning device lifetime, it's crucial to grasp the root causes of device degradation. Environmental factors mentioned earlier, are categorised as 'extrinsic' sources of degradation. These can be mitigated through encapsulation techniques. Unfortunately, intrinsic sources of degradation cannot be addressed using the same methods. Delamination is one example. It is especially prevalent in flexible PVs, such as OPVs, under thermal stress. This leads to the separation of encapsulation layers, thereby compromising the device’s structural integrity, and rendering it vulnerable to extrinsic factors. When OPVs are illuminated, they heat up, facilitating unwanted modification in the device nanostructure. Molecular rearrangements in the active layers or buffer layers lead to segregation at interfaces and hinder charge extraction (Riede, M. et al 2020). The materials in the active layer then undergo phase separation at distances larger than the exciton diffusion length, harming the device’s ability to create free charge carriers, which limits open-circuit voltage (Riede, M. et al 2020). The energy of incident UV light is sufficiently high to break the carbon bonds in the organic material (Riede, M. et al 2020). This causes photochemical reactions within the cell. For this reason, UV light is often considered an ‘intrinsic’ source of degradation. The most harmful region of the solar spectrum can be mitigated through the incorporation of UV filters into encapsulation layers (Riede, M. et al 2020). This method of encapsulation results in improved protection against mechanical stress. Additionally, the self-adhesive backing completes the ready-to-use solar film solution for buildings with restrictions on weight, statics, access, insulation, guarantee and penetration of the roof (Müller, S. and Kube, S. 2020). In fullerene-based OPVs, another source of intrinsic chemical degradation is photo-oligomerisation, especially in C60 and PC61BM. This causes burn-in losses in OPV efficiencies (Riede, M. et al 2020). The efficiency of the respective photovoltaic devices is discussed in the next section but, given the relatively high power conversion efficiencies that have now been reached by OPVs, the search to improve long-term device stability is acquiring a new urgency (Riede, M. et al 2020). These offer more room for reducing the cost of electricity from OPV than further efficiency increases (Riede, M. et al 2020). OPVs might not currently show thirty years of operational lifetime, like polycrystalline silicon (c-Si) cells, but recent studies have demonstrated 100,000 hr operational lifetimes in organic light-emitted diodes (OLEDs). This is indicative that long lifetimes are within reach with OSCs and, hopefully, as a consequence, OPVs (Kippelen, B. and Brédas, J.L. 2009).

4. Energy Efficiency The predicted rise in global energy consumption to 30TW by 2050 raises significant concerns regarding the sourcing of this energy and its associated consequences. Historically, fossil fuels have been favoured for their abundance, but this is quickly becoming redundant. Thus, there is a growing incentive to explore alternative renewable energy sources to meet future energy demands. Solar cells are the perfect solution, provided we can improve the necessary parameters. One of these being efficiency (Darling, S.B. and You, F. 2013). Power conversion efficiency (PCE) is one of the most important device parameters. It describes how effective a PV system is at converting solar energy into electrical energy (Solak & Irmak 2023). Before discussing efficiency in further detail it is important to note that the efficiency of PV devices is notoriously difficult to measure accurately. Additionally, efficiency is not the absolute determinant of the value of PV technology. It does not take into account potential economic, energy and environmental issues (Solak & Irmak 2023). This means we cannot unambiguously determine that c-Si cells are superior. Nonetheless, a certain minimum efficiency is required even if a panel costs close to nothing to produce (Darling, S.B. and You, F. 2013).

OPV modules have a lower embodied energy compared to SiPVs and manage to generate over one hundred times the energy required for disposal, production and use combined over their operation lifespan. Therefore, the use of OPV modules results in net positive energy gain. So, despite having lower efficiencies and shorter lifetimes, they generate more energy than it takes to produce and manage them (McCulloch, I. et al. 2023). OPVs have theoretical efficiencies that are the same as conventional semiconductors. However, the molecular nature of OSCs places restrictions on OPV design. The arrangement of molecules in a typical OSC is disordered, resembling amorphous mixtures more than crystalline lattices, so the conventional description of charge transport cannot be used for OPVs (Shaheen, S.E. et al 2011). Instead, we describe charges as hopping between molecules, meaning it is harder to compare charge transport in silicon and organic modules.

This process is temperature-activated. Increasing temperature causes increased carrier mobility. Conversely, in inorganic semiconductors (ISCs), this dependence is decreasing (Shaheen, S.E. et al 2011). The typical exciton diffusion length in OPVs is in the order of 10 nm due to the weak intermolecular coupling between molecules. This keeps charge carrier mobility low, dictating that the thickness of the active layer is kept thin, which limits optical density and power conversion efficiency (Shaheen, S.E. et al 2011). Despite this, the organic materials used in OPVs are easier to handle than those in all SiPVs (Chidichimo, G. and Luigi, F. 2010). Furthermore, OPVs have a greater band gap than SiPVs meaning that higher energy photons can be absorbed without losing much of the energy to thermalisation (VR, A. 2024). Therefore, they can operate at higher voltages. This is particularly useful in hybrid cells, which incorporate both organic and inorganic technologies. In hybrid cells, the lower energy photons pass through the top organic solar cell and are then absorbed by the inorganic cell at the bottom (VR, A. 2024). As previously mentioned, light absorption across the solar spectrum in OPVs can be maximised using NFAs. These absorb more strongly in the near-infrared region. Y6 is the highest-performing NFA so far with an absorption maximum of around 800 nm, meaning it captures a large portion of the solar spectrum. A decrease in this value increases non-radiative decay, limiting device efficiency (Lowrie, W. et al. 2023). OPVs are composed of several thin layers of material which have varied optical properties. This means there is occasionally a mismatch in refractive indices at multiple interfaces leading to reflections that produce optical interference effects and an uneven light distribution inside the cell. This is determined by a complicated interplay of optical constants depending on the material and its thickness. Knowledge of light distribution in the solar cell is important to model charge carrier distributions and, as a result, charge carrier distributions (Kippelen, B. and Brédas, J.L. 2009). High OPV efficiencies can be achieved by fabricating tandem solar cells with multiple active layers and different band gaps (VR, A. 2024). In turn, a greater fraction of the solar spectrum is captured. In such devices, high-energy photons are absorbed by the first layer, which has the largest band gap, while lower-energy photons pass through to the second layer for capture by a material with a lower band gap (Darling, S.B. and You, F. 2013). Incorporating conjugated oligomers and polymers leads to absorption bands that are very intense due to the large wave-function overlap between the ground state and lowest excited states. These bandwidths allow for good matching with a sizable portion of the solar spectrum (Kippelen, B. and Brédas, J.L. 2009). Although SiPVs exhibit higher efficiencies compared to OPVs, the options for what materials we can incorporate into OPVs are endless, whereas those for use in SiPVs are limited. Our ability to manipulate OPVs in such a way means that OPVs have exceptional low-light performance and higher efficiencies under low-light conditions compared to SiPVs, making them more suitable for use in regions with less sunlight, or for indoor application (Solak, E.K. and Irmak, E. 2023).

5. Commercial Applications OPVs are still under development and have a long way to go before they can establish a position as dominant as that of silicon technologies. When comparing the respective technologies, we must be careful since some technologies may potentially not be able to achieve technical goals in the end, as they may run into unexpected hurdles in their development. Currently, SiPVs are the dominating technology for power plants. c-Si technology benefits from a strong market position and a large active research community. However, as research efforts continue and efficiency improves as a consequence, we begin to approach their theoretical efficiency limit (Heinrich, M. et al 2020). Silicon modules tend to suffer from exceptionally high capex which has significant implications on both the per-unit profitability and scalability of the PV industry (M. Powell, D. et al. 2015). If we are to reduce module cost, we must reduce the amount of silicon used, as the material cost is the largest contributor to the overall cost. Unfortunately, if we continue to thin wafers, we must develop advanced wafer handling capabilities to manipulate these fragilely thin wafers (Tao, M. 2008). The integration of solar cell technology in different applications demands a new set of requirements, which differs from those applied in PV power plants because certain design qualities are more important than the device parameters previously discussed (Heinrich, M. et al 2020). OPVs provide the opportunity to deliver electricity at a much lower cost (Riede, M. et al 2020). So, although silicon solar cells may be dominating in PV power plants, outside this OPVs thrive. The lightweight, flexible and variable angle performance means that OPVs are incredibly versatile and well-suited for niche markets where SiPVs cannot meet the requirements (Mazzio, K.A. and Luscombe, C.K. 2014). One example includes urban settings where building walls represent a larger amount of unusable area than rooftops (Darling, S.B. and You, F. 2013). The low-light performance of OPVs, an efficiency which does not suffer with increasing temperatures, their low weight and superior ability to capture incident light from different angles means they are well-suited to wall applications and can even be mounted using double-sided sticky tape, providing additional space for solar technology that would otherwise be inaccessible if one were limited to the usage of inorganic materials (Darling, S.B. and You, F. 2013). Additionally, the flexibility of OPVs means they can be stored and packaged more easily and their low weights means they are easy to transport and can even be delivered to areas of the globe which have limited access to standard on-grid electricity (Zhang, X. et al. 2012). In agriculture, OPVs can be used in power irrigation systems and crop monitoring systems and in transportation, they can be used to power electric vehicles, boats and drones (Solak, E.K. and Irmak, E. 2023). It is easy to conclude that in areas where efficiency is not the main focus, thin-film technologies, such as OPVs, show significant advantages. The versatile nature of OPVs is unmatched by any other solar technology (Heinrich, M. et al 2020).

Conclusion The research dictates that OPVs do have the potential to match their silicon competitors. However, the evidence does not indicate that OPVs will ever fully replace silicon technologies. Silicon solar cells are highly effective in regions with high electricity prices and ample sunlight. Their impressive efficiencies, which do not suffer in the transition to the commercial market, mean that silicon is an established, well-grounded technology. However, their cost makes them less competitive against fossil-fuel-generated electricity (Darling, S.B. and You, F. 2013). With rising costs and limited availability of alternative energy sources, the population may be forced to accept higher-cost solar electricity. Therefore, the transformative potential of low-cost solar cell technologies, such as OPVs, should not be underestimated (Tao, M. 2008). The energy-intensive manufacturing of SiPVs is a large contributor to the overall module cost. This process relies heavily on fossil fuels which may impede their effectiveness at reducing fossil fuel usage. Additionally, SiPVs present significant disposal challenges. OPVs are made of decomposable materials that can be incinerated to produce energy sufficient for further OPV production in a closed loop. The time required for an OPV to return the sum of the energy required to both manufacture and decommission the device is substantially lower compared to SiPVs. So, in terms of energy sustainability and manufacturing, OPVs are far superior. (Mazzio, K.A. and Luscombe, C.K. 2014). Furthermore, a vast array of molecules can be incorporated into organic modules meaning, we can easily manipulate device parameters via the changing of molecular arrangements. Thus, the research indicates that the exponential growth we have seen so far will continue with the same propensity in other device parameters. The computational analysis suggests if we were to succeed in transitioning OPVs with laboratory-level PCEs to the commercial market, they could directly compete with c-Si panels, even for home usage. Hence, the primary challenge that lies ahead is conserving the efficiency gains seen in the lab. As we allocate a larger proportion of government funding to researching renewable energy technologies, an increase in PV implementation is to be expected. Solar cells stand out as they do not damage the surrounding landscape or rely heavily on environmental factors like wind. OPVs present promising opportunities for innovation due to their diverse range of applications, positioning them favourably for commercial success by fulfilling unique needs. However, silicon technologies maintain advantages in efficiency, lifespan, and stability so are expected to remain dominant in industrial power plants, especially given their established status. This leads to the conclusion that the most likely direction for the PV industry is to enhance the development of hybrid cells, combining silicon and organic materials to leverage the primary advantages of each technology and best reconcile our desire for high efficiency and low eco-toxicities to decarbonise our future

and meet the rapidly increasing energy demand. The distinct applications of each technology suggest that they will most likely coexist, rather than compete. Although it is unlikely that SiPVs will be replaced at industrial scale, OPVs will evolve to surpass SiPVs in all other settings. Morphology refers to the arrangement and organisation of the various components within the active layer of the solar cell. The active layer typically consists of a blend of organic materials, such as polymers or small molecules, that are responsible for absorption light and generating charge carriers when exposed to sunlight. (The charge carriers in PV devices are holes and electrons). The morphology of the active layer is crucial for the efficiency of a solar cell. The spatial distribution and arrangement of the donor and acceptor materials within the active layer significantly influence the exciton dissociation, charge transport and charge collection processes. (Excitons are electron-hole pairs). Optimal morphology ensures that excitons can efficiently diffuse to the donor-acceptor interfaces where charge separation occurs. Band gap is the energy difference between the highest energy electrons in the valence band and the lowest energy electrons in the conduction band of a material. It determines the energy of photons that a material can absorb and the energy required to generate charge carriers when exposed to light. For photovoltaic materials smaller band gaps mean that the materials can absorb lower-energy photons and therefore the fact that OPVs have a high band gap and therefore can only absorb higher energy photons limits their ability to convert sunlight into electrical energy and will be less efficient in harvesting lower-energy photons, such as those in the infrared part of the solar spectrum.
